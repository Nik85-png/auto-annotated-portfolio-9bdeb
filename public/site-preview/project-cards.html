Core question

What separates successful problem-solvers from those who struggle? The task is deceptively simple: arrange a set of playing cards on an 8×8 grid. But the paths participants take through that grid — whether they plan, whether they group by rank, whether they use blank cards as scaffolding — look very different depending on how the trial ends.

This project takes the log of every card movement and treats it as the primary data. Outcomes (completion, time) are secondary. The question is what the trajectory looks like — and whether the shape of the trajectory predicts the outcome.

Method

Participants used an interactive 8×8 grid to arrange Kings, Queens, Jacks, and — in some conditions — blank cards. Each movement was tracked with:

  • timestamp
  • source cell → destination cell
  • card identity
  • trial condition (with / without blanks)

The analysis quantifies spatial organization (how grouped the final layout is), strategy quality (whether movements reflect a plan or scrub-and-retry behaviour), blank-card usage (scaffolding vs. idle placement), and success (completion status on the trial).

"The fastest way to describe a struggling participant is: they move a card, then move it back, then move a neighbour card, then move the first card again. Successful participants look different on the grid even before they're done."

What the data showed

01 — Organized layouts correlate with completion

Trials that ended in a low-messiness layout (cards grouped by rank, aligned on rows or columns) were far more likely to be scored as successful. This is partly tautological — the task rewards structure — but the effect is visible well before the trial ends, in the interim snapshots.

02 — Strategy quality adds explanatory power

Organization alone isn't sufficient. Two participants can reach the same final layout via very different paths. Strategy quality — roughly, the ratio of "forward" moves (placing a card in its final region) to "undo" moves — adds predictive power beyond time-on-task or final organization score alone.

03 — Blank cards behave like scaffolding, not noise

In conditions with blank cards, successful participants used blanks as placeholders for cards they hadn't yet decided on — i.e. as working memory externalized onto the grid. Unsuccessful participants tended to treat blanks as filler or ignore them entirely.

Caveats I want in writing

  • Sample size is modest, and the participant pool is student-heavy. I'd be careful generalizing to other populations without a replication.
  • "Messiness" is a derived metric; reasonable people could define it differently. The repo documents the exact formula so alternative definitions are easy to swap in.
  • Causal direction is not settled. Organized layouts predict success, but organized participants may also be faster planners — the trajectory data helps but doesn't resolve this.

What I'd do next

A pre-registered replication with a broader participant pool, plus a version of the task that collects a short verbal protocol after each trial — so strategy quality can be validated against a participant's own description of what they were doing.
